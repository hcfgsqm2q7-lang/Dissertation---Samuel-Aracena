# Source-level table, plain-language version

A simplified rewrite of the notebook's "Source-level table: how each mechanism actually
observes Somalia" (`src/Dissertation_master_file.ipynb`, Next steps item 7), for quick
reference. The notebook version stays in formal academic language for the dissertation
itself; this one exists so the same content is easy to re-read at a glance. Kept alongside
the original for the notebook restructuring review.

| | **Conflict (ACLED)** | **Climate (rainfall + vegetation)** | **Market (WFP prices)** | **Reporting (ReliefWeb)** |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| **How is the data collected?** | People compile lists of violent events mostly from Somali news outlets and local partners. About a third of events don't even say where the information came from. | Not collected by people at all — satellites measure it automatically every week/month, everywhere, the same way. | WFP staff physically visit markets each week and write down prices. | Aid organizations (UN agencies, NGOs, the government) write and publish reports whenever they want to. |
| **Where could it theoretically tell us something?** | Anywhere — in theory, a violent event anywhere in Somalia could get picked up. | Anywhere — satellites cover the whole country the same way regardless of what's happening on the ground. | Only in the 35 districts (out of 74) that actually have a market being watched. | Anywhere — a report could mention any district, there's no physical requirement. |
| **Where does data actually exist?** | Everywhere, every month — 100%. | Everywhere, every month — 100%. | About 47% of district-months have a price recorded (but only 43% have WFP's "how unusual is this price" score — that gap turned out to be a real finding). | About 60% of district-months have at least one report; 73 of 74 districts get mentioned at least once across the year. |
| **What does a "0" mean?** | "No violence was recorded here this month" — but that could mean nothing happened, or it could mean something happened and nobody caught it. | A real, meaningful number (e.g., "no rain this month"), not a gap in the data. | There's no such thing as a real price of $0 — if you ever saw one, it would mean something went wrong, not that prices are free. | "Nobody wrote about this place this month" — but again, that could mean truly nothing happened, or it could mean something happened and just didn't get picked up. |
| **What does "missing" mean?** | Basically never missing — it's always filled in with a number, even if that number is 0. | Never missing, ever, for the whole year. | "This market didn't report a price for this item that month" — either because there's no market there at all, or the market skipped that one item. | Never technically "missing" either — it's always a number, so 0 is the only "nothing here" signal for this source. |
| **What are the biggest weaknesses?** | It reflects where journalists/monitors are paying attention, not where violence is actually happening — remote areas get undercounted. | The most trustworthy of the four since it doesn't rely on anyone reporting anything, but not perfect: rain estimates are shakier in areas with few weather stations, and cloud cover can interfere with the vegetation readings. | Markets get chosen for monitoring because they're big, safe, and easy to reach — meaning the most dangerous, hardest-to-reach places are exactly the ones missing from this data. | The weakest of the four: the text-matching can mismatch names, only understands English/Somali, can't read image-based reports (infographics/maps), and — something we found ourselves — tends to write a lot more about conflict than about slow-building crises like drought. |
