## New points explored

- 772 of 3,448 conflict events (that is, 22% of them) are only approximately located rather than pinned to the exact event location, with substantial variation between districts. This fits the paper extremely well because it demonstrates that even apparently precise event data contain an important layer of measurement uncertainty.
- This finding also exposes a problem in the planned granularity description. Write something like this: "*ACLED is event-level and geocoded to latitude/longitude, but geographic precision varies and approximately 22% of Somalia 2024 events are coded at an approximate rather than exact location*".
- About 8.8% of basket-commodity price observations have an actual price but no corresponding PEWI value, concentrated mainly in three districts. This means “market coverage” depends on which variable is being used: approximately 47.2% if price availability defines coverage versus 43.1% when PEWI availability defines it. This suggests you should stop using a single generic term such as “market coverage” unless you specify what it exactly means.
- Derived features can amplify missingness. Price itself is missing 52.8% of the time, while its month-to-month trend is missing 56.8% because two consecutive observations are required. This is a useful general data-engineering point: feature engineering can reduce effective coverage even when it adds analytical richness.
- In 99.4% of district-months, WFP either provides all four commodities or none. This tells us something about the underlying collection protocol rather than about individual commodities and is worth reporting.
- The last new idea is to study whether different sources agree about where crisis conditions occur, not merely whether they cover the same district-months. This is useful, but I would treat it as secondary. The preliminary conflict-versus-market-stress association is borderline and introduces another question: conflict and market stress measure different mechanisms, so lack of agreement is not necessarily a data-quality failure. They are not expected to identify exactly the same places. 

## Paper narrative

Humanitarian researchers increasingly combine heterogeneous datasets, but those datasets are generated through fundamentally different observation processes. Before using them together, we need to understand what each source can observe, where it is missing, what biases its collection process introduces, and what is gained by integration.

### Research questions

Possible main research question:

> How do heterogeneous humanitarian data sources differ in their spatial-temporal coverage, granularity and measurement limitations, and what does their integration reveal about systematic biases in humanitarian data collection?

Three sub-questions would be:

- RQ1. How does data availability differ across collection mechanisms?
- RQ2. Are gaps and reporting attention systematically associated with particular places or crisis signals?
- RQ3. What additional information becomes available when heterogeneous sources are integrated?

That gives the paper a coherent progression:

1. How the sources collect.
2. What they fail to collect.
3. Whether the failures are systematic.
4. What integration contributes.

### Strongest findings

1. The pseudo-replication analysis is robust and scientifically valuable. It demonstrates concretely how a naïve analysis of an integrated humanitarian panel can generate a false positive simply because districts appear repeatedly.
2. The conflict-versus-vegetation-stress reporting difference is also increasingly convincing within Somalia in 2024. It survives multiple thresholds, a continuous severity analysis, restriction to food/nutrition reports, delayed reporting and district-cluster correction. I would now consider this a genuine result, although the interpretation should remain narrow: "*ReliefWeb reporting attention in Somalia during 2024 is much more strongly associated with recorded conflict intensity than with vegetation stress*".
3. I would avoid broader statements such as “*humanitarian organisations neglect climate crises*”, because the study measures one reporting platform, one country and one year, and VHI captures vegetation stress rather than the full humanitarian impact of drought.
4. The market/reporting complementarity result is also solid descriptively. It shows clearly that the two systems contribute different district-month coverage, while ReliefWeb dominates at the coarser question of whether a district is represented at all.
5. The geoparser precision result is promising, but I would not yet call all of its reported recall/F1 results robust because of the stratified validation sampling issue described above.

## Weaknesses

### 1. The WFP forecast problem remains the largest unresolved issue

This is the point I would resolve before any further market analysis. My previous feedback explicitly asked for an observed/forecast audit because you had previously discovered extensive forecast contamination. The current presentation does not show this audit.

If March–December 2024 market values are forecasts rather than observations, then several current statements about market “data collection” could be invalid. Forecast availability is not collection coverage. We needs to settle this conclusively.

### 2. The study currently has only one country and one year

For the MSc dissertation this is not a problem at all, especially because the work is very well done. For a strong research paper, however, this limits external validity. We do not know whether the conflict-reporting asymmetry or ReliefWeb/WFP complementarity is particular to Somalia.

I would not solve this next week by suddenly implementing four countries. That would be risky at this stage. Instead, write the dissertation as a rigorous Somalia case study. If the work is later prepared for publication, we can later replicate the framework across Ethiopia, Kenya and South Sudan.

### 3. “Climate stress” should be named more precisely

The headline comparison is effectively conflict versus vegetation-health stress, because VHI is the climate-side variable used in the analysis. That is narrower than “climate crisis” or “drought severity”.

I would therefore use "conflict intensity versus vegetation stress", unless we combine CHIRPS rainfall anomaly and VHI into a defensible drought measure. Do not invent a composite index now unless strongly justified.

### 4. Temporal and seasonal confounding is not completely addressed

Your current result may partly reflect seasonal differences between months rather than different reporting responses to conflict and vegetation stress. Run one multivariable model including conflict intensity, vegetation stress and month controls, while accounting for repeated districts. If conflict remains the stronger predictor of reporting attention, this substantially strengthens the interpretation that the reporting system responds more strongly to conflict than to vegetation stress.

A clean final robustness model could be:

$$Reporting_{i,t} = f(Conflict_{i,t}, VegetationStress_{i,t}, Month_t)$$

with:

- $i$ is the district;
- $t$ is the month;
- $Conflict$ measures conflict intensity;
- $VegetationStress$ measures VHI stress;
- $Month$ is included as a control.

If the reporting–conflict association remains substantially stronger than the vegetation association after month controls, the substantive conclusion becomes cleaner. Because report counts are count data and probably overdispersed, a negative binomial model may be more natural than ordinary linear regression.

### 5. Some supposedly final documentation is still internally inconsistent

Two examples are important:

- ACLED cannot simultaneously be described as “exact GPS” data and as having 22.4% approximately located events.
- `market_stress_count` should not retain a name that we know is incorrect.
