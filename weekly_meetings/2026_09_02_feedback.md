## Paper narrative

### 1. Opening argument and research questions

Humanitarian crisis analysis increasingly relies on combining heterogeneous geographic data sources such as conflict-event databases, satellite observations, market-monitoring systems and humanitarian reports. These sources are often treated as compatible inputs to downstream models, while the integration process itself is considered a technical preprocessing step.

This study starts from a different position: *data integration is itself a methodological problem that can materially affect scientific conclusions*.

The sources used here differ in how they observe reality, at what spatial and temporal resolution they operate, how missing values arise, how locations are represented, and whether observations are genuinely measured or model-generated. The Somalia case study shows that these differences are not minor implementation details. They can affect coverage estimates, engineered features, statistical significance and the apparent relationship between crisis signals and humanitarian attention.

The main research question can therefore be framed as:

> *What methodological challenges arise when integrating heterogeneous geographic humanitarian data for crisis analysis, and how can these challenges be systematically identified and mitigated?*

This can be supported by three sub-questions:

- *RQ1. Data integration and quality:* How do humanitarian sources differ in spatial granularity, temporal resolution, missingness, provenance and measurement uncertainty?
- *RQ2. Failure modes:* Which integration choices can introduce misleading coverage estimates, invalid features or incorrect statistical conclusions?
- *RQ3. Empirical consequences:* What does applying a source-aware integration framework to Somalia reveal about humanitarian observability and reporting bias?

This framing keeps the paper focused on the general methodological contribution, while using Somalia as the empirical case through which the framework is tested.

### 2. Prior work

There is substantial prior work arguing that humanitarian data are fragmented, heterogeneous and difficult to integrate. Bell et al. [1], for example, explicitly identify integration of diverse humanitarian datasets as a major challenge and argue that integration is often not considered early enough. Earlier work also proposed a validated humanitarian geospatial data model for systematic collection and management, while HXL was developed specifically to improve interoperability and information exchange.

There are also existing frameworks around humanitarian data ecosystems and data preparedness [2]. Likewise, OCHA's HDX Data Grids already assess humanitarian data availability according to criteria such as subnational resolution, common formats, timeliness and comprehensiveness [3]. This means that a contribution framed simply as “we measure humanitarian data coverage” would not be novel.

And importantly, bias and missingness in humanitarian data are already recognised research topics. Marshak et al. explicitly argue that humanitarian missingness is often structural rather than random. Paulus et al. identify political, accessibility, topical and sampling biases in crisis information management in Yemen [3]. Conflict-data researchers have likewise studied reporting bias and systematic omission in event databases [4].

### 3. Contribution

1. *An executable source-level validation framework integrated directly into the data-integration pipeline*. Existing work discusses interoperability, data ecosystems, preparedness and bias largely at the conceptual, organisational or source-specific level. Samuel takes several fundamentally different observation infrastructures (event monitoring, remote sensing, physical market surveys, humanitarian reporting) and forces them onto the same Admin-2 × Month analytical grid, while explicitly auditing what is lost or distorted during that transformation. That enables him to distinguish operationally: (i) structural availability; (ii) recorded availability; (iii) zero versus missing; (iv) observed versus forecast; (v) native versus harmonised spatial resolution; (vi) location precision; (vii) feature-induced missingness; (viii) extraction failure versus information absent from machine-readable text. The novelty is therefore not any one of these concepts. It is their joint operationalisation as part of a reproducible integration workflow, demonstrated on real humanitarian sources.
2. *A taxonomy of integration failure modes demonstrated empirically*. Individual problems are not new. For example, conflict reporting bias is known, structural humanitarian missingness is known, interoperability problems are known. But we show how these problems surface concretely when datasets are actually harmonised, and in several cases demonstrates that they alter the result. Examples include: pseudo-replication turning \(p=.188\) into the false \(p=.0001\); a supposedly geographic conflict-density measure mostly reproducing administrative-area effects; PEWI coverage differing from price coverage; derived temporal features increasing missingness; ReliefWeb geolocation being limited not mainly by aliases but by information trapped in attachments; ACLED coordinates appearing precise while 22% have only approximate location precision; forecast contamination needing to be audited at the exact country–commodity–time slice rather than inferred from the source globally. This is a credible contribution because we do not merely list best practices. We supply observed failure → diagnostic → correction → changed interpretation. That could be one of the paper's main tables.
3. *Cross-infrastructure comparison of humanitarian observability*. HDX already measures whether categories of humanitarian data are available and current (https://centre.humdata.org/data-services/). We instead compare what happens when different observation mechanisms watch the same geography and time: (i) satellites; (ii) conflict monitoring; (iii) market enumerators; (iv) organisational reports. That lets us ask: What does “coverage” mean under different observation technologies? This sociotechnical comparison is potentially interesting because a satellite's absence, a market survey's absence, an ACLED zero and a ReliefWeb zero are fundamentally different phenomena. I'm not aware about any study conducting our exact cross-source, common-grid empirical audit of those four observation mechanisms. That looks like a genuine niche.
4. *The conflict-versus-vegetation attention result is probably novel in its specific form*. There is strong prior literature on topical bias, accessibility bias, reporting bias and “streetlight effects”. Paulus et al. (https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S0268401223000440) explicitly identify issue/topic bias in humanitarian crisis information, and climate-conflict scholarship has documented accessibility and selection effects. So the broad claim “humanitarian attention is biased” is definitely not novel. But our specific result seems materially more precise: *Within the same subnational humanitarian reporting system, and controlling for month, reporting volume responds strongly to recorded conflict intensity but shows essentially no measurable response to vegetation stress; the asymmetry remains within Food & Nutrition reports*. I'm not aware about any existing study making that specific quantitative comparison for ReliefWeb. That can therefore be presented as a new empirical case-study result, but not as discovery of humanitarian attention bias in general. It is especially valuable because it gives quantitative support to mechanisms discussed more qualitatively in the crisis-information-management literature. Paulus et al., for example, identify topical and accessibility biases using interviews/documents; we provide a computational subnational measurement of something closely related. That is a nice interdisciplinary bridge.

I would write the paper's contributions approximately like this:

> **First**, we introduce an operational, source-aware audit workflow for integrating heterogeneous humanitarian geospatial data at a common spatial-temporal resolution. Unlike approaches focused primarily on interoperability or dataset availability, the workflow explicitly audits how each source's observation process affects zeros, missingness, provenance, spatial precision and derived features.

> **Second**, through its application to four distinct humanitarian observation infrastructures, we identify and empirically document a set of integration failure modes that can materially change downstream conclusions. These include provenance contamination, spatial-precision loss, structural versus derived missingness, text-extraction blind spots and pseudo-replication.

> **Third**, we show how harmonising heterogeneous sources makes cross-infrastructure observability measurable, revealing when sources provide complementary coverage and where high-stress district-months remain absent from human-generated information systems.

> **Fourth**, in a Somalia 2024 case study, we find that ReliefWeb reporting responds substantially more strongly to recorded conflict intensity than to vegetation stress, including within Food & Nutrition reporting, providing quantitative evidence of crisis-type-dependent visibility.

### 4. Main Results

**Result 1: Different sources have fundamentally different observation structures**

The four data sources differ substantially in how they observe space and time.

- Climate data provide near-complete spatial coverage through satellite observation.
- Conflict data are event-based and geographically detailed, but location precision varies and unreported events cannot be distinguished from genuine zero-conflict observations.
- Market data depend on monitored and accessible markets and therefore contain structural geographic gaps.
- ReliefWeb reporting depends on organisational attention and what information is available in exported text.

This means that a common Admin-2 × Month table does not imply that all sources measure reality with comparable completeness or reliability.

**Result 2: Apparently simple preprocessing decisions can change scientific conclusions**

Several initially reasonable modelling decisions were shown to be problematic.

- Area-normalised conflict density was strongly driven by district size and added little beyond event counts, so it was removed.
- Percentage-change variables were unstable when previous values were small or zero and were replaced by log-difference measures.
- Most importantly, the original full-panel test suggested a highly significant association between market coverage and conflict. Once the repeated district structure was handled correctly, the effect disappeared.

This provides a concrete example of how integration choices can generate false scientific findings.

**Result 3: “Coverage” is not a single concept**

Market availability provides a clear example. The study distinguishes: (i) market-presence coverage; (ii) price coverage; (iii) PEWI coverage. These differ because some districts have markets but not complete observations, and some price observations lack PEWI values. Similarly, ACLED's complete analytical panel does not imply complete real-world observability, because a zero may represent either no event or no recorded event.

**Result 4: Humanitarian reporting is much more responsive to conflict than vegetation stress**

The negative-binomial model provides the strongest substantive result. After standardising predictors and controlling for month:

- a one-standard-deviation increase in conflict intensity is associated with approximately *69% more expected ReliefWeb reports*;
- vegetation stress has approximately no measurable effect on reporting attention;
- the conflict and vegetation-stress coefficients are significantly different.

When the analysis is restricted to Food and Nutrition reports, the pattern remains: conflict continues to predict substantially greater reporting attention, while vegetation stress remains weak.

**Result 5: Severe crisis signals can occur in human-generated information blind spots**

The study identifies 27 district-months across 18 districts where neither WFP market data nor ReliefWeb reporting is available despite relatively extreme recorded conflict or vegetation stress. Most of these blind spots are associated with vegetation stress rather than conflict. This provides a concrete example of how remote-sensing or event data can reveal stress in locations that are poorly represented in human-generated information systems.

### 5. Robustness of results

The strongest empirical result (i.e., the conflict-versus-vegetation reporting asymmetry) has been tested in several ways. It survives:

- different definitions of extreme observations;
- continuous rather than threshold-based comparisons;
- district-clustered uncertainty estimates;
- month controls;
- exclusion and penalised inclusion of Banadir;
- restriction to Food and Nutrition reports;
- delayed-reporting checks.

The reporting-attention result is therefore reasonably robust within Somalia in 2024.

The data-integration findings are also strengthened by several explicit validation exercises:

- the WFP observed-versus-forecast audit was performed on the exact analytical slice used in the dissertation;
- the ReliefWeb geoparser was evaluated on a fresh blind-labelled sample;
- recall estimates were corrected for the stratified sampling design;
- repeated-observation artefacts were explicitly identified and corrected;
- feature definitions and formulas were documented and revised where necessary.

However, we should avoid claiming causality. The reporting analysis shows differential association with crisis signals, not that humanitarian organisations deliberately prioritise conflict.

### 6. Limitations

1. *Single-country, single-year case study*. The empirical results are based on Somalia in 2024. This is sufficient to demonstrate the methodological framework, but it limits external validity. The finding that reporting responds more strongly to conflict than vegetation stress should therefore be stated narrowly: "ReliefWeb reporting in Somalia during 2024 responds more strongly to recorded conflict intensity than to vegetation stress". Replication across Ethiopia, Kenya and South Sudan would be the natural next step for a future publication extension.
2. *ReliefWeb recall is structurally limited*. The geoparser has reasonably strong precision, but recall is limited because much geographic detail is contained in PDFs, tables and attachments not present in exported text. Therefore, the ReliefWeb feature should be interpreted as district-level information visible in exported ReliefWeb text, not as complete district-level reporting coverage. This limitation may itself be crisis-type dependent and should be acknowledged as a possible alternative explanation for part of the reporting asymmetry.
3. *Vegetation stress is not equivalent to humanitarian drought severity*. VHI captures vegetation stress, not the complete humanitarian consequences of drought. Therefore, the paper should use terms such as "vegetation stress", rather than "climate crisis severity" or "equivalent drought severity".
4. *Persistent district-level confounding may remain*. District-clustered standard errors correctly address repeated observations, but they do not remove all persistent differences between districts, such as accessibility, population, NGO presence or political importance. A district fixed-effects robustness model would further strengthen the headline reporting result if feasible.
5. *Different data sources measure different constructs*. Conflict, vegetation stress, market conditions and reporting are not interchangeable indicators of one latent crisis variable. Lack of agreement between them should therefore not automatically be interpreted as data error. In many cases, complementarity is scientifically more appropriate than agreement.

### 7. Implications of This Study

1. *Implication for humanitarian data science.* Researchers should not treat data integration as a neutral preprocessing step. This study shows that provenance, native spatial resolution, missingness mechanisms, location precision and repeated spatial observations can materially change downstream results. The practical implication is that "integrated humanitarian datasets should undergo a source audit before modelling".
2. *Implication for reproducible crisis analysis*. Reproducibility should include the full data-generation and integration process, not only the final modelling code. Future studies should clearly document whether values are observed or model-generated, what zeros and missing values mean, how precise geographic observations are, whether engineered features reduce effective coverage, whether geographic matching has been independently validated, and whether repeated spatial units are handled correctly. This source-audit workflow can become one of the paper's most reusable outputs.
3. *Implication for humanitarian information systems*. The Somalia case suggests that human-generated reporting may respond much more strongly to acute conflict than to slow-onset vegetation stress. If this pattern is replicated elsewhere, it would suggest that humanitarian information infrastructures themselves contribute to differences in which crisis signals become visible. This has implications for early warning, anticipatory action, humanitarian monitoring and resource allocation.
4. *Implication for machine learning and prediction*. Predictive models can only learn from what the underlying information system observes. If particular places or crisis mechanisms are systematically under-observed, downstream models may reproduce those blind spots even when the modelling methodology is technically sound. "Model quality cannot be separated from observation quality". Before improving prediction models, researchers should first understand how the underlying data were generated and what they may systematically fail to observe.
